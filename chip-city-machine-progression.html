Research becomes gameplay

Three steps from idea to production

An engineering project grants knowledge. The player must still fund a line and prove that it can produce qualified components.

1

Develop capability

Spend cash and engineering capacity after the company has demonstrated demand, shortages, quality problems, or supplier exposure.

2

Purchase and build

Unlocked machines enter the catalog. The player commits capital, floor space, logistics, and supporting inspection or test capacity.

3

Qualify output

Run pilot batches and produce enough accepted samples at the required quality before internal output can feed normal production.

Suppliers stay relevant. Unlocking internal production never removes purchased equivalents. Supplier backup, partial integration, and external purchasing remain valid strategies.

Deliberate first-release boundary

One campus. Three internal verticals.

The first game should prove deep factory decisions in one expandable site before adding every component family or inter-factory freight.

TH

Thermal components

The accessible first integration branch.

  • Continuous forming and finishing
  • Bulky material logistics
  • Capacity grades
  • Recyclable offcuts
MEM

Memory

The first wafer-production branch.

  • Wafer batches
  • Spatial yield variation
  • Parallel die output
  • Speed and quality bins
SUB

Substrates and links

The package-material planning branch.

  • Panel nesting
  • Product-size allocation
  • Valuable offcuts
  • Shared X-ray inspection
Deferred, not deleted: I/O, power, and compute remain purchasable; internal production for those families, multiple sites, freight networks, and upstream materials are expansion directions. Advanced packaging remains in scope because it deepens final assembly without requiring another die-fabrication vertical.

Candidate teaching sequence

How complexity arrives

The campaign suggests problems in an understandable order while leaving later specialization open to the player.

1

Purchased assembly

Buy every component and ship a basic package.

Learn: chip design creates factory demand.

2

Flow improvement

Supplier deliveries and queues begin limiting output.

Learn: routing and buffers matter.

3

Quality pressure

Reject loss becomes expensive enough to address.

Learn: inspect before adding more value.

4

Thermal pilot

Heat-spreader cost and freight justify simple integration.

Learn: production changes space and logistics.

5

Memory pilot

Memory spending justifies the first wafer line.

Learn: batch yield trades simplicity for margin.

6

Hybrid supply

Internal memory cannot satisfy every product.

Learn: buy and make can coexist.

7

Package materials

Several products require different substrates and links.

Learn: panel waste and product mix interact.

8

Advanced product

A contract needs dense links or higher reliability.

Learn: capability unlocks design space.

9

Integrated campus

Several departments feed multiple final products.

Learn: shared capacity creates leverage and contention.

Equipment progression

Later does not always mean better

Machine generations add capabilities and tradeoffs. Older equipment remains useful for prototypes, small batches, and low-utilization lines.

100

Foundation series

Affordable and compact.

  • Modest throughput
  • Limited recipe set
  • Small local buffers
  • Good for prototypes and dedicated lines
200

Automation series

Efficient when kept busy.

  • Larger footprint
  • Higher capital and power
  • Advanced recipes
  • Stronger shared-factory economics
300

Frontier series

Specialized capability, not a universal upgrade.

  • Enables new products
  • Demanding infrastructure
  • Difficult maintenance
  • Wasteful for basic production
