The central idea

The chip does not prescribe the factory.

The design creates requirements and pressure. The player decides how to satisfy them through sourcing, layout, routing, capacity, and quality.

1 Choose parts

Select compute, memory, I/O, power, cooling, and connections to meet a product opportunity.

2 Generate pressure

The design becomes a bill of materials plus placement, bonding, inspection, test, and packaging workloads.

3 Build a network

Route purchased or internally produced items through machines with enough capacity and valid outputs.

4 Ship accepted chips

Throughput, yield, component cost, operating cost, and reject loss determine whether production is profitable.

Manufacturing profile: the bridge between the chip designer and factory. It describes what production must handle, but never dictates one correct layout.

Throughput

  • Each stage has a setup time and workload capacity.
  • The slowest effective stage controls attempted packages per minute.
  • Transport congestion, starvation, or blocking can reduce actual output.
attempts/min = 60 / largest effective cycle time

Economics

  • Every reject loses purchased parts and all processing already completed.
  • More machines can raise output while worsening cash position.
  • A higher-performing chip earns more only when an opportunity pays for it.
profit/min = accepted/min x margin per accepted chip

Prototype versus full game: the browser experiment uses memory as its first internal-production test. The proposed full campaign introduces simpler thermal production first, then memory as the first wafer branch, followed by substrates and interconnects. The first release stays on one expandable campus; I/O, power, compute, upstream materials, and multi-site freight remain expansion directions.
